# Review the game as a designed place

An art-direction label or a passed geometry test does not make a scene attractive. Inspect actual
player views. If the current result is unattractive, change composition and assets before claiming
quality; adding bloom, particles or a menu over the same weak world is insufficient.

Before implementation, record the user's direction and a small palette, material/shape language,
reference images and three important compositions: arrival, normal play and payoff. Use assets
with distinct silhouettes at deliberate scales. Models may come from vetted CC0/CC BY libraries;
record provenance and match their style. Parts are useful construction tools, not an excuse for
rows of identical sphere-topped trunks or flat discs pretending to be finished scenery.

Compose a foreground, a readable play space and a framed destination. Give areas distinct silhouettes,
colour accents and density; leave deliberate empty space around jumps. A winding route, reveal or
vertical landmark can do more than hundreds of randomly scattered props. Vary clusters, rotate and
scale assets deliberately, and avoid equal-spacing every object. Keep decorative collision off.
Use the player's camera to judge scale: giant boards, oversized HUDs and a foreground hill blocking
the route are failures even if their world coordinates are correct.

Review title/menu, tutorial, each stage and finish in context, with UI and the actual avatar. Check
hierarchy, text wrapping, contrast, native touch controls, scenery/route separation, draw distance,
load pop-in, lighting and animation. Compare new captures with the baseline and the chosen references.
State remaining weaknesses candidly. A screenshot showing a colourful level is not proof of polish.

The completion report must distinguish: mechanics tested, journey tested, art visually reviewed,
and account/live-service limitations. A prototype may be a successful prototype; label it accurately.
